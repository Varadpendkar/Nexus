The big idea

What classification is

Supervised learning means the computer learns from examples that already come with the right answer attached. That attached answer is called a label, and examples with labels are labeled data — like flashcards with the answer printed on the back.

Classification is the supervised task where the answer is a category — a named bucket — never a number. The computer looks at something new and picks which bucket it belongs in.

Rule of thumb: if the answer is a name, it's classification. If the answer is a number, it's regression — the next lesson.
02

The learning loop

How a classifier learns

You show the computer many labeled examples — the training data. It hunts for patterns that separate the buckets. The pattern it ends up with is the model (the learned pattern itself — not the recipe that found it). Then the model makes guesses, called predictions, on new examples it has never seen.

Training data

Labeled examples: "this one is spam, this one is not…"

→

The model

Finds the pattern that separates the buckets.

→

New, unseen data

A fresh email arrives — the model predicts its bucket.

Why hold some examples back? Think of school. Practice tests are for learning; the final exam is for judging. Grade a student on a practice test they already memorized and the score tells you nothing. So the data is split: most becomes the training set (practice), and a held-back part becomes the test set (the final exam). You only trust the final exam score.

?Try it: predicting tomorrow's temperature — 31 degrees — is that classification?
No. 31 degrees is a number, not a category. Predicting numbers is regression. Classification would be predicting a bucket like hot or cold.
03

The toolbox

Meet the classifiers

A classifier is the recipe (the algorithm) that learns the pattern. Different recipes have different styles. These six names come up again and again:

AlgorithmThe one-line ideaShines when…
Logistic RegressionDraws the best straight dividing line between the buckets.You want a simple, fast, explainable start.
k-NN (k nearest neighbors)Asks the nearest examples: "what are you?" and copies the majority.The dataset is small and groups form natural clumps.
Decision TreeA flowchart of yes/no questions that ends in a bucket.You want to see the reasoning, step by step.
Random ForestBuilds many decision trees and lets them vote.You want accuracy and don't mind a black box.
Naive BayesFast probability math: "how likely is each bucket, given these words?"Text — spam filters are its classic home.
SVM (support vector machine)Draws the widest possible boundary between the groups.Groups are close and every bit of margin matters.

Each of these has its own full explainer in the ML catalogue — just ask in chat, e.g. "explain decision trees", and that lesson is one message away.

The geometry of sorting

Decision boundaries

Plot every email as a dot, its two features as map coordinates. The model's decision boundary is the line or curve it draws on that map: dots on one side get one label, dots on the other side get the other. The boundary is the model's whole opinion, drawn as a line.

The key intuition: a wigglier boundary fits the training dots better — it can wrap around every one. But the tighter it hugs the practice dots, the less you should trust it on new dots, which never land in exactly the same places. Hold that thought — it's mistake #1 in §07.

Grading the model

Is it any good? The confusion matrix

One score hides the story. The confusion matrix is a 2×2 table: what the model predicted against what was actually true. Its four boxes:

Actually spam
Actually not spam
Predicted spam
42 · True PositiveFlagged as spam — and it was. Caught. ✓
6 · False PositiveFlagged as spam — but it was good mail. A false alarm. ✗
Predicted not spam
4 · False NegativeLet through — but it was spam. A miss. ✗
48 · True NegativeLet through — and it was good mail. Correct. ✓

Worked numbers (100 test emails): correct = 42 + 48 = 90, so accuracy — the share of all predictions that were right — is 90%. But accuracy blends both kinds of mistake. Two sharper questions split them apart:

· Precision: of the mail flagged as spam, how much truly was? → 42 of 48 flagged ≈ 88%.
· Recall: of all real spam, how much got caught? → 42 of 46 real spam ≈ 91%.

The tradeoff, in one line: an aggressive filter catches all the spam (perfect recall) — but jails good mail with it (precision crashes). You can't max both; you choose which mistake hurts more.

07

Where beginners get burned

The classic mistakes

Mistake 1 — Overfitting: memorizing the practice test. The model's boundary wiggles around every training dot until the practice score is perfect — but new mail lands in the wiggles' blind spots and gets sorted wrong. Memorizing is not learning.

Mistake 2 — The imbalanced-data trap. Imbalanced means one bucket dwarfs the other. Say 99 of 100 emails are "not spam." A lazy model that always answers "not spam" — catching literally zero spam — scores 99% accuracy. The score looks brilliant; the filter is useless. That's exactly why §06 gave you precision and recall: on lopsided data, they tell the truth accuracy hides.

Rule of thumb: never trust accuracy alone on lopsided data. Ask for precision and recall before you believe any score.
?Try it: a tumor test is right 99% of the time, but only 1 in 100 tumors is malignant. Impressed?
No. A "test" that always says benign also scores 99% — and misses every dangerous tumor (recall = 0). On lopsided data, demand precision and recall, not accuracy.

Keep these

Pocket rules

  • Classification = predicting categories from labeled examples.
  • Train on practice, judge on the final exam — the test set is the only score you trust.
  • The boundary is the model's opinion, drawn as a line.
  • Accuracy lies on lopsided data — check precision and recall.
  • Wiggly boundaries memorize; smooth ones generalize.
  • Start simple (logistic regression) before you go fancy.

Prove it to yourself

Check your learning

Five questions on the ideas that matter. Answer all five, then press Submit.

1. Sorting mail into spam / not spam — classification or regression?

Classification. Spam / not spam are named buckets. Regression is for answers that are numbers, like a temperature.

2. What does "labeled data" mean?

Labels are the answers. Each training example carries its correct bucket — that's what makes the learning "supervised."

3. A model scores 99% accuracy on mail that is 99-to-1 "not spam." Trust it?

Not trustworthy. On lopsided data, accuracy rewards doing nothing. Precision and recall expose a filter that catches zero spam.

4. Which line has precision and recall the right way round?

Precision judges the flags; recall judges the catch. Flag carefully → precision. Catch everything → recall. You trade one against the other.

5. Which classifier asks its nearest examples what they are, and copies the majority?

k-NN. It stores the examples and, for each new one, asks the k closest neighbors to vote. Logistic regression draws a line; SVM draws the widest one.
