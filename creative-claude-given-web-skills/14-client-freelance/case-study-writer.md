# SKILL: case-study-writer
**Layer:** 14 — Client & Freelance
**Goal:** write credible, outcome-oriented case studies that explain context, constraints, decisions, implementation, and verified results without exaggerating evidence.

---

## Why This Matters (Senior Perspective)
A portfolio case study can undermine trust when it shows polished mockups but hides the actual problem, presents team work as solo work, or invents conversion lifts without analytics. Prospective clients need to understand how the freelancer thinks, handles constraints, and makes trade-offs—not merely whether a screenshot looks attractive. Confidentiality also matters: publishing client data, internal dashboards, or unreleased designs without permission can damage relationships. Strong case studies state the role, evidence, limitations, and outcome clearly, then connect decisions to the problem rather than writing a gallery caption.

## Relevant Patterns / Integration Patterns / Advanced Patterns

### Pattern 1: Problem-decision-result arc
What it does: connects the original pain to a decision and its observable effect. When to use: most project narratives.

**Implementation:**

```markdown
## Problem-decision-result arc
- Context: describe the client situation and evidence.
- Action: state the next concrete step and its owner.
- Boundary: identify what is excluded or uncertain.
- Verification: define the observable acceptance check.
- Record: log the decision and any approved change.
```

**Operational note:** Use this pattern when its trigger is present; do not apply it mechanically when it adds ceremony without reducing meaningful uncertainty. Confirm the client's understanding before treating the step as approved.

## Common Mistakes

| ID | Mistake | Unique corrective action |
|---|---|---|
| A | Vague deliverables | Replace broad nouns with countable outputs and acceptance evidence. |
| B | Unverified assumptions | Label each assumption and assign a validation owner. |
| C | Guaranteed business results | Describe controllable deliverables and qualify outcome uncertainty. |
| D | Unlimited revisions | Define rounds, consolidation rules, and change-request handling. |
| E | Missing mobile behavior | Specify supported viewports and test critical flows on small screens. |
| F | Hidden third-party dependency | Document account owner, access, sandbox, quota, and failure path. |
| G | No approval owner | Name the authorized approver and the response deadline. |
| H | Unclear payment trigger | Tie invoices to dated milestones or accepted deliverables. |
| I | No out-of-scope route | Provide a written change-request path with estimate and approval. |
| J | Unverified portfolio claim | Link claims to evidence and label personal or concept work. |
| K | No content ownership | Assign responsibility for copy, images, rights, and final approval. |
| L | Testing treated as optional | Include explicit QA tasks and release criteria. |
| M | Ambiguous launch responsibility | Specify who owns DNS, hosting, backups, and deployment access. |
| N | Support confused with warranty | Separate defect correction from enhancements and ongoing maintenance. |
| O | No decision log | Record approved choices, open issues, and who decided. |
| P | Privacy ignored | Minimize prospect/client data and obtain permission for sensitive publication. |

## Mental Models

1. **Scope is a boundary, not a mood.** A friendly conversation does not replace a written deliverable definition.
2. **Evidence beats confidence.** State what is known, inferred, and still to be tested.
3. **Every uncertainty has a cost.** Resolve it, price it, or explicitly exclude it.
4. **A proposal is an operating interface.** It aligns expectations before implementation begins.
5. **Client feedback is input, not automatically a requirement.** Clarify the goal and evaluate impact.
6. **Trust compounds through accurate claims.** A modest verified result is stronger than an invented one.
7. **Quality includes the handoff.** A feature is incomplete if nobody can operate or maintain it.
8. **No decision is free of ownership.** Each approval, asset, and dependency needs a responsible person.

## Build Challenge

### Build: Creative Web Case Study Writing — Client-Ready Simulation
Write a case study for a redesign where no reliable conversion analytics exist, the work was shared with a designer, and the client cannot be named. Use only verifiable facts, distinguish measured results from qualitative feedback, and create a useful narrative without inventing percentages or claiming sole ownership.

### Required deliverables

1. Write a concise problem statement and identify the intended audience.
2. Separate verified facts from assumptions and unresolved questions.
3. Define deliverables with quantities, owners, and acceptance evidence.
4. Identify the approver and a practical feedback deadline.
5. Build a milestone plan that includes discovery, implementation, QA, and handoff.
6. Estimate effort or impact using explicit work packages rather than an unexplained total.
7. State exclusions, dependencies, and the process for scope changes.
8. Include accessibility, responsive behavior, and critical failure states in verification.
9. Draft client-facing communication that is direct, respectful, and free of fabricated claims.
10. Add a decision log with status, owner, date, and next action.
11. Simulate a failure: a required asset, approval, or third-party service is unavailable.
12. Explain how the plan changes and what must be approved before work resumes.

### Definition of done

- [ ] The brief or proposal names a concrete outcome and avoids unsupported guarantees.
- [ ] Every deliverable has a boundary and a testable acceptance condition.
- [ ] Assumptions and dependencies are visible, assigned, and validated or explicitly unresolved.
- [ ] Commercial and schedule impacts are explained before scope changes are accepted.
- [ ] The failure scenario has a documented mitigation and decision owner.
- [ ] The final artifact can be understood by a client who missed the discovery conversation.
- [ ] Claims about experience, metrics, ownership, and results are accurate and attributable.

### Review checklist

- Can a reader distinguish fact from assumption?
- Could two reasonable people interpret scope differently?
- Does the next step have an owner and deadline?
- Are costs and risks disclosed without fear or pressure?
- Is the response useful if the client declines or delays approval?

### Field Note 1: Applying Creative Web Case Study Writing in a Real Engagement

In practice, creative web case study writing should reduce ambiguity rather than create paperwork for its own sake. Begin with the smallest decision that changes delivery risk. Ask for evidence, record the answer, and explain what remains uncertain. If a stakeholder asks for an immediate commitment before evidence exists, offer a bounded next step such as a discovery task, prototype, estimate range, or written option instead of presenting a guess as certainty. Consider how this decision affects the client's users, internal approvers, technical dependencies, delivery schedule, and future maintenance. A strong professional artifact is concise enough to use but complete enough that another person can continue the work without reconstructing the original conversation.

```text
Situation: A client request conflicts with the agreed brief.
1. Restate the requested outcome without judgment.
2. Compare the request with recorded acceptance criteria.
3. Identify effort, schedule, quality, and dependency impact.
4. Offer options with trade-offs and a recommendation.
5. Wait for explicit approval before changing the baseline.
6. Update the decision log and retest affected behavior.
```

**Reflection prompt:** What would a future maintainer, client approver, or independent reviewer need to understand why this decision was made? Include the original need, evidence available at the time, accepted trade-off, and condition that would cause the decision to be revisited. Avoid using a polished document to disguise missing information; instead, state what is known and name the next action that will reduce uncertainty.
